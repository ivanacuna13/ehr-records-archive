# Why I built this

*Ivan Acuña*

My biggest constraint with AI isn't the AI. It's me turning what I can see in my head into words, and then hoping they're the right words, the ones that turn into the right lines of code.

Most of the time what I actually know is "I'll know it when I see it." That's tacit knowledge. I can't always name it. Every new domain, whether that's robotics, medicine or data, makes me learn a whole new vocabulary before the AI will give me what I'm picturing. Even in fields where I'd call myself somewhat of an expert, it's not because I know every word for every thing. But the words are what you have to hand to the machine.

Humans aren't built for walls of text. We're built for space and movement. Athletes, anyone who uses their body, we understand shape before we understand sentences. Think about making a peace sign. You don't need to read the firing pattern of every neuron in your arm to know it's wrong. You just look and see you used the wrong fingers. Then someone says "lift your pointer and middle, hold the other two down with your thumb," and it's fixed. The neurons still fire. You just don't have to know which ones.

So this archive is a test of that idea. Data has to be structured in a uniform, repeatable way for anything to work. That's what schemas and data models are. If the structure is real, it can have a real shape. Here, a hospital's records become a library: chapters are wings, patients are charts, visits are folders, diagnoses are rings running through time. Nothing is decoration. Every shape comes from the data.

When the shape is honest, you can diagnose by looking. And a mess is worth seeing too. If papers are everywhere but you can see how they relate, you can clean it up, the same way you clean your room. A messy system could be rebuilt against a clean blueprint like this one, with the uncertain pieces lighting up for a human to check.

Where I want to take it is agents and automation. Point it at a repo or a workflow, run it, and watch the agent move through the structure instead of reading its logs. When something breaks, you'd see where. Then you'd say "connect this piece to that one" and let the computer translate the movement back into code.

This first version is built on Synthetic Hospital, an open, fully synthetic EHR benchmark by Christine Park, Valerie Chen and Tim Dettmers at Carnegie Mellon. It helps me see the data, and it helps me think. I hope it does the same for you.
